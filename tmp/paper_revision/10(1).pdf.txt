ABSTRACT
The key to successfully cold starting proton-exchange membrane fuel cells is increasing the cell temperature
above 0 °C before the electrochemical reaction stops, because ice forms in fuel cells below this temperature. To
decrease the input of external heat energy to fuel cells during cold starting, this paper proposes a local-heating
method to improve the cold-start performance of fuel cells. During the experiments, heating wires were placed
under partial ridges in the cathode plate to improve the cold-start performance of the fuel cells. The cold-start
characteristics of the locally heated fuel cells were analyzed by measuring the voltage, high-frequency im-
pedance, and cathode (gas di ﬀusion layer) temperature for di ﬀerent heating power densities and number of
heating wires. The results show that locally heating the cathode improves the cold-start capability of the fuel
cell, and increasing the heating power density to heat the fuel cell enhances the voltage stability during cold
starting of the cell. Furthermore, at a constant heating power density, the fuel cell using one heating wire shows
better cold-start performance than that heated using three heating wires.
1. Introduction
Proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are widely used for
their cleanliness and high energy conversion rates [1]. Simultaneously,
improving the cold-start ability of the PEMFCs used in vehicles is im-
portant to the application of such cells in environments below 0 °C. Ice
formation in these PEMFCs during cold starting separates the catalytic
layer (CL) and gas di ﬀusion layer (GDL) and decreases the reaction
areas [2], thereby lowering or even stopping the electrochemical re-
action rate [3]. After multiple freeze –thaw cycles in the PEMFCs, pla-
tinum particles in the CL are removed, and the fuel-cell impedance
increases [4]. In addition, volume expansion owing to water freezing in
the cathode membrane electrode causes other problems in the cells.

To successfully cold start fuel cells in a cold environment (below
0 °C), Florian et al. [8] used methanol as a cold-start antifreeze for
PEMFCs; however, methanol impairs the normal temperature perfor-
mance of fuel cells. Jiang et al. [9] and Ko et al. [10] studied the e ﬀect
of current density on the cold-start performance. They believed that
increasing the initial current density not only accelerates the increase in
fuel-cell temperature but also increases water accumulation in the
cathode CL, easily resulting in cold-start failure. Amamou et al. [11]
proposed a real-time adaptive cold-start strategy of fuel cells to control
the operating current in real time and quickly cold start fuel cells. Luo
et al. [12] and Lin et al. [13] determined that the more the number of
single PEMFC, the faster the temperature of the fuel-cell stack rises, and
the better is the cold-start performance. Although the PEMFC can
generate more heat energy when operating at a normal temperature
[14], when the ambient temperature is low, utilizing the heat generated
by an electrochemical reaction to increase the fuel-cell temperature for
a successful cold start is di ﬃ cult [15]. Phase-change materials can store
a large amount of latent heat [16] and can be used as microscale heat-
transfer materials [17]. Therefore, for short-time shutdown of fuel cells,
the latent heat of phase change can be used as a heat source. Using
numerical calculations, Sasmito et al. [18] found that fuel-cell stacks
can be successfully started from −20 °C when a phase-change material
is used as a heat-storage medium. Furthermore, external heating
sources, which mainly include a circulating heating medium [18], he-
ated reaction gas [19], thermoelectric device [20], and external com-
bustion, can help e ﬀectively cold start PEMFCs [21]. Zhang et al. [22]
also proposed a cold-start method in which PEMFCs are heated using a
heating medium circulating around the fuel cells. Li et al. [23] wrapped
heat insulation materials around a fuel-cell stack while circulating a
heating medium to preheat the stack during cold starting. Although
these methods heat the PEMFCs, the use of a circulating heating
medium can result in a steady increase in the fuel-cell temperature;
these methods also consume a large amount of heat energy and prolong
the cold-start process because the circulating heating medium and fuel
cells simultaneously consume heat energy. In addition, to decrease the
volume of the fuel-cell heat-exchange system, Guo et al. [24] and Jiao
et al. [25] showed that using air as a medium increases the intake gas
temperature and decreases the current density loss in fuel-cell cold-start
simulation. Nguyen et al. [26] proposed a heat-recovery system by
preheating the intake air, obviously improving the heat-utilization rate
for heating the fuel-cell stack. Khandelwal et al. [27] estimated that
heating anode gas has a high heat-utilization rate as hydrogen has a
larger heat transfer coe ﬃ cient than air. However, heating the reaction
gas to increase the fuel-cell temperature still utilizes a large amount of
heat energy and prolongs the cold-start time as the reaction gas has a
low heat capacity. Although external combustion can quickly increase
the temperature of PEMFCs [28],
 the use of a burner considerably in-
creases the volume and weight of the fuel-cell system and results in
uneven fuel-cell temperature [29]; such a system is unsuitable for cold
starting PEMFCs in vehicles. Zhou et al. [30] used an electric heater to
heat a fuel-cell stack during a simulated cold-start process and were
able to successfully cold start the stack at −20 °C. Thompson et al. [31]
proposed a patent to place heating wires in PEMFCs to help cold
starting but did not conduct corresponding experimental investigations.
The existing heating modes for cold starting PEMFCs were com-
prehensively analyzed. Although the electrical-heating-based cold-start
mode has many advantages, such as ﬂexible control, simple add-on
devices, and high heating e ﬃ ciency, the direct use of heating wires for
heating PEMFCs during the cold-start process has rarely been studied
experimentally. In particular, the local-heating-based cold-start modes
lack corresponding cold-start experimental and theoretical analyses.
Therefore, in this study, a local-heating-based cold-start method, in
which heating wires are placed under cathode ridges, was experimen-
tally analyzed to successfully cold start PEMFCs while inputting less
external heat energy. The local heating method is used to rapidly in-
crease the temperature of the partial membrane electrode above 0 °C
using a small amount of heat energy to maintain a continuous elec-
trochemical reaction in this region. Then, the reaction heat released in
this partial region can further increase the temperature in other reac-
tion zones above 0 °C to successfully cold start fuel cells. Changes in the
voltage, impedance, and cathode GDL temperature were analyzed to
study the e ﬀect of the local heating method on the cold-start capability
of PEMFCs for various heating power densities and di ﬀerent numbers of
heating wires in the fuel cell.
2. Experimental devices and system
2.1. Design of the locally heated fuel cell
Fig. 1 shows the locations of heating wires placed under ridges in
the cathode and the local heating principle underlying the cold starting
of fuel cells. The central reaction zone of a PEMFC has the highest
current density in the fuel cell that easily causes a vacuum e ﬀect from
starvation [32] and is crucial to a successful cold start [33]. To heat the
membrane electrode assembly (MEA) using external heat energy, we
placed heating wires in the center of the fuel cell. In addition, to
compare the e ﬀects of concentrated and dispersed local heating and to
prevent the shape of the bipolar plate from a ﬀecting the fuel-cell per-
formance, di ﬀerent arrangements of heating wires were employed.
Speciﬁcally, either one wire was employed in the center of the fuel cell
or three wires were employed, i.e., one in the center and two identical
heating wires under the ridges on either side ( Fig. 1(a)). Fig. 1(c) shows

study. In the experiment, the reaction area of the fuel cell was 25 cm

to the heating wires through the polar plate, current collector, and end
plate were connected to the DC power supply. In addition, the anode
and cathode comprised a heat-insulation layer between the current
collectors and end plates, respectively, to decrease heat loss in the re-
action zone.
2.2. Experimental system
Fig. 3 shows the experimental system for cold starting the locally
heated fuel cell. The electrochemical workstation (Interface 5000E)
whose frequency was 2500 Hz was used to measure the fuel-cell im-
pedance during the cold-start process, and its lowest scanning time was
10 μs. Before entering the fuel cell, both hydrogen and air were cooled
to the experimental temperature ( −10 °C) by cooling the coils in the
cryogenic box ( Fig. 3). The DC power supply (ITECH-IT6720) could
supply a maximum power of 100 W to the heating wires. In addition,
the control accuracies of the output voltage (0 –60 V) and output current
(0–5 A) were < 0.05% and < 0.2%, respectively. A data-acquisition
recorder (YOKOGAWA-MV1000) was used to measure and record the
cathode GDL temperature, and the measuring accuracy of the thermo-
couples ( K-type) was < 0.1 °C. The fuel-cell test system (BATE-FC100)
had a voltage measurement range of 0 –2 V, and its accuracy was ±
0.1% FS. After fuel-cell operation, any water remaining in the fuel cell
can seriously a ﬀect the next cold-start performance [34]. Therefore, the
fuel cell was purged using nitrogen [35] for 1 h at 25 °C before cold
starting to remove water from the cathode and anode sides of the fuel
cell [36]. After purging, the fuel cell was cooled to the experimental
temperature ( −10 °C) and continued cooling for 3 h. At the beginning
of the cold-start process, the heating wires in the cathode were powered
simultaneously, and the voltage, impedance, and cathode GDL tem-
perature were tested.
As the ridges with the heating wires were wider than the other
ridges, the ability to remove water may be decreased. In addition, the
heat-insulation material ﬁlled in the wide ridges could increase the
contact resistance between the GDL and bipolar plate in the cathode.
Therefore, analyzing the e ﬀect of the locally heated bipolar plate on the
normal temperature performance of the fuel cell before cold starting is
important. Table 1 lists some experimental conditions for analyzing the
normal-temperature and cold-start performance of the locally heated
fuel cell.

3. Experimental results and discussion
3.1. E ﬀect of locally heated bipolar plate on the normal temperature
performance of the PEMFC
As the structure of the cathode bipolar plate changed after installing
heating wires, experiments were conducted to analyze the curren-
t–voltage (I–V) performance (60 °C) of the locally heated fuel cell with a
locally heated bipolar plate before cold starting. The voltage and power
density of the PEMFC using two bipolar plates (normal and locally
heated bipolar plates) changes with increasing current density, as
shown in Fig. 4. Two fuel cells were tested three times each with a
maximum experimental error of no more than 3%. The type of the
cathode bipolar plate had little e ﬀect on the fuel-cell output voltage
until the current density reached 0.9 A cm
−2. After the current density
exceeded 1.0 A cm −2, the output voltage of the fuel cell using the bi-
polar plate with heating wires was lower than that of the fuel cell using
the normal bipolar plate. As three wide ridges existed in the locally
heated bipolar plate, the path through which air reached the cathode
CL under the wide ridges increased; in addition, purging water from the
GDL under the wide ridges was di ﬃ cult. Therefore, air was prevented
from di ﬀusing into the CL under the wide ridges, thus decreasing the
voltage at high current densities. However, the power density curves
show that the peak power density of the fuel cell using the locally he-
ated bipolar plate decreased by approximately 0.016 W cm −2 at 1.0 A
cm−2. This means that normal-temperature performance loss of the
locally heated fuel cell isless than 3%. Therefore, the locally heated
bipolar plate hardly a ﬀects the practical utilization of the PEMFC.
Nevertheless, to decrease the voltage loss caused by this bipolar plate,
the width of the ridges with the heating wires can be decreased to
improve the normal-temperature performance of the fuel cell.
3.2. E ﬀect of local heating on the cold-start performance of PEMFC
This section compares the cold-start performance of the locally
heated fuel cell under di ﬀerent heating power densities with the self-
starting performance of the fuel cell. Only one heating wire at T1
(Fig. 1(a)) was used to heat the fuel cell under three heating power
densities ( Table 1) during the cold-start process.
The voltage, impedance, and temperature T3 ( Fig. 1(a)) changed for
locally heated and self-started fuel cells from −10 °C at three heating
power densities (P1/P2/P3; Table 1), as shown in Fig. 5 (a), and from
−20 °C at two heating power densities (P'1/P'2; Table 1), as shown in
Fig. 5 (b). Fig. 5(a) shows that approximately 500 s after cell start-up,
the voltage of the fuel cell without local heating decreased to 0 V. When
the heating power density was 47.0 W kg
−1, the fuel cell ran con-
tinuously for approximately 800 s until the voltage dropped to 0 V.
Conversely, when the heating power density reached 52.2 W kg −1, the
fuel-cell voltage did not decrease to 0 V before its overall temperature
increased above 0 °C, indicating a successful cold start. However, the
voltage was not stable during the cold-start process for low heating
power density. That is, when the heating power density was at 60.6 W
kg
−1, the total temperature of the fuel cell increased above 0 °C, 200 s
before that for the heating power density of 52.2 W kg −1. In particular,
at 60.6 W kg −1, the PEMFC voltage was relatively stable. The afore-
mentioned experimental results clearly illustrate that the locally heated
membrane electrode could improve the fuel cell ’s cold-start capability.
For the self-starting fuel cell, the impedance continued to decrease for
approximately 200 s from the start-up, as shown in Fig. 5 (a), because
the PEM was wetted with water produced in the cathode CL to decrease
ion transfer resistance. As the electrochemical reaction continued,
water that could not be absorbed by the PEM began to ﬂow into the
interface between the CL and MPL in the cathode. Water at the CL/MPL
interface in the cathode froze after approximately 200 s, increasing the
interface electrical contact resistance. Therefore, the fuel-cell im-
pedance increased at that time. For the locally heated fuel cell at a low
heating power density (47.0 W kg
−1), the impedance increased after
approximately 300 s, illustrating that local heating prolonged the
starting time of ice formation. However, the fuel cell eventually failed
to cold start owing to the gradually rising temperature. When the
heating power density was increased to 52.2 W kg
−1 after 300 s from

3.3. E ﬀect of number of heating wires on cold-start performance
When the heating power density is the same, the temperature-ﬁ eld
distribution in the reaction zone is a ﬀected by the number of heating
wires in the cathode. This change in temperature- ﬁeld distribution may
in turn a ﬀect the cold-start performance of the fuel cell. Therefore, the
cold-start capability of the fuel cell was analyzed using one and three
heating wires under the same total heating power density.
The changes in the voltages, impedances, and cathode GDL tem-
peratures for the fuel cells using one and three heating wires during the
cold-start process from −10 °C are shown in Fig. 6 for a heating power
density of 52.2 W kg
−1. The fuel cell with one heating wire initiated
successfully from −10 °C, while the voltage of the fuel cell using three
heating wires dropped to 0 V after 1080 s, as shown in Fig. 6(a). This is
because the fuel cell using one heating wire had better cold-start cap-
ability than that using three heating wires. Fig. 6(b) shows that in the
ﬁrst 200 s, the fuel cell using one heating wire showed a greater de-
crease in impedance than that using three heating wires, implying that
the former had better wetting ability on the PEM. The temperature at
T1 (central position of cathode GDL) for the fuel cell using one heating
wire increased above 0 °C after approximately 230 s, and certain non-
frozen areas existed in the central reaction zone. As the temperature in
the central reaction zone continued to increase, these nonfrozen reac-
tion areas also constantly expanded until the temperature at T3 (posi-
tion at the edge of the cathode GDL) increased above 0 °C after
approximately 1100 s. However, for the fuel cell using three heating
wires ( Fig. 6(d)), the temperatures at T1 and T2 increased above 0 °C
after approximately 400 and 850 s, respectively. In particular, the
temperature at T3 remained under 0 °C until the fuel cell failed to start.
This is because every wire was powered with less amount of heat to
locally heat the membrane electrode under the wide ridges. Therefore,
the cathode GDL temperature near the heating wires increased gradu-
ally, resulting in the formation of more ice in the cathode. In contrast,
in the fuel cell using one heating wire, the wire was powered at a large
heat density under the condition that total heating power was constant.
Moreover, the central reaction zone generated relatively less ice, as the
temperature near the heating wire rapidly increased above 0 °C. The
central zone continued with the electrochemical reaction and released
heat power to heat other reaction zones. Finally, the fuel cell using one
heating wire successfully cold started from −10 °C. Fig. 6(a) shows that
the voltage of the PEMFC using one heating wire dropped considerably
after approximately 1100 s, started to increase after approximately
1150 s, and stabilized after approximately 1200 s. However, the voltage
after 1200 s showed a loss of 0.06 V relative to that before 1100 s. As
shown in Fig. 6 (c), the temperature at T3 increased above 0 °C after
approximately 1150 s, indicating that the ice in the entire reaction zone
melted. We deduce that the ice accumulating in the membrane elec-
trode covered a large area of the reaction zone, thus causing a con-
centration polarization in voltage. Then, the reaction zone previously
covered by ice started to react again after approximately 1150 s owing
to the melting ice. Thus, the voltage started to increase at that time.
However, the melted water at the CL/MPL interface in the cathode
could not be removed quickly enough, so the voltage still showed a
concentration polarization of 0.06 V.

near the heating wire showed a greater ion-transfer rate because of its
higher temperature. In contrast, for the fuel cell with three heating

4. Conclusions
In this study, a local-heating method was proposed for cold starting
a PEMFC by placing heating wires under the partial ridges in the
cathode bipolar plate. In addition, the voltage, high-frequency im-
pedance, and cathode GDL temperature for the fuel cell were measured
from the initial temperature of −10 °C or −20 °C to study the e ﬀects of
diﬀerent heating power densities and number of heating wires on the
cold-start capability of the fuel cell. The main conclusions are as fol-
lows.
(1) Local heating apparently improves the cold-start ability of the fuel
cell. The greater the local heating power, the faster the temperature
of the reaction zone increases, which is conducive to successfully
cold starting a PEMFC. Moreover, compared with other external
heating methods (e.g., heating the circulate water or cathode air),
directly heating the fuel cell cathode MEA enabled higher energy
eﬃ ciency during cold starting.
(2) Insu ﬃ cient local heating power density causes unstable voltage and
even failure of the cold-start process.
(3) At a constant heating power density, the fuel cell using one heating
wire showed better cold-start performance than that using three
heating wires. This means that concentrated heating had better ef-
fect than dispersed heating, and quickly increasing the local tem-
perature was conductive to a successful cold start.
The local-heating method in this study can be applied to cold start a
PEMFC stack for vehicles. Fuel-cell stacks can be rapidly cold started by
arranging a certain number of heating wires in the stack to directly heat
a partial region of each MEA and setting the power of heating wires
according to the ambient temperature.
