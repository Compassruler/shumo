abstract
In this study, a mathematical model is established to simulate the cold start of fuel cell,
including the calculation of the temperature distribution and heat exchange. Moreover, a
novel cold-start mode is designed and compared with the constant and linear current cold-
start modes. It uses the ice volume and heat absorbed by the membrane as fuzzy control
inputs and outputs current density. Compared with other modes at 263 K, the cold startup
time is shortened by 25.6 e41.6 s, and the ice volume fraction is reduced by 29.4% e31.8%.
Only the proposed mode achieves a successful cold start at a lower temperature. Also, the
proposed mode has better thermal behavior, as indicated by the temperature distribution
diagrams. Furthermore, to avoid performance degradation caused by cold starts, an inertia
link is added to the controller, so that the current amplitude is reduced by 7.98%, and the
maximum change rate by 57.44%.

Introduction
Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) have the ad-
vantages of excellent power density, high efﬁciency, and low
emissions. Accordingly, they have been developed and
applied in various ﬁelds [ 1e3]. However, if the temperature of
the external environment is below zero, the water produced
by PEMFC is likely to form ice in the porous medium and cause
startup failure; this has become one of the important factors
hindering the commercialization of PEMFC [ 4e6]. The cold
start of PEMFC consists in starting the fuel cell from a sub-zero
temperature and running to the operating temperature. Dur-
ing this period, the heat and ice generated by the fuel cell
compete with each other [ 7]. If the fuel cell temperature fails
to rise above the freezing point before the ice covers the re-
action area, it will stop working and the cold start will fail.
This will cause irreversible loss to the PEMFC components and
performance [8e10], thus it is essential to fully understand the
principles of cold start and design an effective start mode.

Numerous studies have been concerned with the mecha-
nism and characteristics of cold start through extensive sim-
ulations. Meng et al. [ 11] developed a transient multiphase
multi-dimensional PEMFC model in a mixed-domain frame-
work, whereby it was demonstrated that the water vapor
concentration inside the cathode gas channel affected ice
formation in the cathode catalyst layer. Yao et al. [ 12] inves-
tigated the freezing mechanisms of super-cooled water and
ice formation using a transient three-dimensional multiphase
model, and introduced a freezing probability function. Jiao
et al. [ 13] observed that the ice in the cathode catalytic layer
(CL) was formed faster at the section close to the bipolar plate
(BP) at a lower current density, and it was close to the mem-
brane at a higher current density. Gwak et al. [ 14] explored ice
melting and transient water transport characteristics as well
as the resultant dynamic responses under various operating
conditions in terms of the air ﬂow rate, external thermal
boundary condition, inlet RH, and startup temperature.
Ouyang et al. [ 15] proposed a dimension-reduction simpliﬁ-
cation method to obtain a model with a lower computational
cost. Amamou et al. [ 16] experimentally demonstrated that
the model proposed by Amphlett had higher accuracy than
the Squadrito and Boulon models in terms of the prediction of
characteristics under above and below zero temperature
conditions. Khandelwal et al. [ 17] investigated the effective-
ness of various warming strategies in reducing the start-up
time by developing a one-dimensional thermal mathemat-
ical model. Zhou et al. [ 6] developed a one-dimensional cold-
start model; furthermore, a novel start-up method, namely,
variable heating and load control (VHLC), was proposed and
evaluated based on this model. One-dimensional models are
suitable for calculating the heat generation of a fuel cell and
the mutual heat transfer of its internal structure. In particular,
when the ice volume fraction is calculated without consid-
ering its speciﬁc distribution, a one-dimensional model can
better balance the modeling details and calculation efﬁciency.
By considering various cold-start modes, several schemes
have been proposed to optimize the cold-start process
[18e25]. Du et al. [ 18] demonstrated that, compared with
constant voltage and constant current modes, the maximum
power cold start mode could better balance heat generation
and ice, thus improving cold start survivability. Lin et al. [ 19]
studied the cold start characteristics of the PEMFC stack in the
constant voltage start mode. They found that the stack had
better cold start performance than a single fuel cell and ach-
ieved a fast cold start within 95s at a temperature of /C0 15
/C14 C.
Luo et al. [ 20] clariﬁed the fundamental difference between
the constant power, constant current and constant voltage
cold start modes, and observed that the heating effect had
little bearing on the start mode, which was mainly determined
by the current density. They recommended that maintaining a
high current density during cold start was always beneﬁcial to
stack heating. Jiang et al. [ 24] used a lumped thermal model
and proposed a current-ramping strategy for rapidly starting
up PEMFCs from a subfreezing environment. It has been
demonstrated that changing the cold-start mode such that a
larger amount of heat and a lower ice volume fraction are
generated can improve the start-up capability of the fuel cell.
However, the minimum PEMFC cold start temperature in
these modes is approximately /C0 10
/C14 C. This is because the
relationship between heat generation and ice is ignored when
the current density and voltage are changed. It is premised
that the current density must be changed under full consid-
eration of the thermal behavior so that the cold start mode can
be better formulated.
Moxley et al. [ 26] presented the ﬁrst quantitative analysis
of ignition in PEMFCs and found their steady-state multiplic-
ity. They cleverly attributed the main contradiction between
water production and removal to the diversity problem.
However, there is no further study on the cold start method
and mode, and the effect of uneven internal temperature
distribution is ignored.
In this study, a numerical model considering multiphase
heat and mass transfer is established to simulate the cold
start process. Through this model, we can calculate the ice
volume fraction, temperature distribution, heat production,
and heat exchange between the layers of the PEMFC.
Furthermore, the present study uses ice volume and heat
absorbed by the membrane as fuzzy control inputs, and out-
puts the current density so that a cold start mode is designed.
Compared with other schemes, this cold start mode uses the
inﬂuence of thermal behavior as the basis of the fuel cell
output and provides new insights into the design of a cold
start strategy. The cold start process of the PEMFC is analyzed
according to the temperature distribution of the fuel cell
under different ice volumes or membrane heat absorption.
The numerical results clearly indicate the difference in ther-
mal performance between the proposed cold start mode and
other modes at low temperatures. The control effect of the
inertia link is also investigated. The paper is structured as
follows. Numerical mode aims at the numerical model, which
forms the basis of the entire study. Results and discussion
validates the model and proposes a new cold start mode. The
simulation results of various modes are also compared. The
major conclusions are stated in the last section.
Numerical model
Basic assumption
The establishment of the model is the key to the research on
the cold start of PEMFC. The quantitative formulation of heat
transfer and water transmission is fundamental and should
be clariﬁed ﬁrst [ 15,17]. The following assumptions are made
in this analysis:
(1) Each layer is one-dimensional during the operation of
the fuel cell, and its temperature is uniform. It can be
assumed that the membrane electronics assembly
(MEA), gas diffusion layer (GDL), BP, gas channel and
cooling channel have uniform (but different) tempera-
ture distributions. The MEA is composed of the mem-
brane (MEM) and CL.
(2) The produced water can be frozen in the cathode CL and
GDL, but only the ice in the CL of the cathode blocks the
reaction area. Because the thickness of the CL is a few
microns, the invalid reaction area caused by ice block-
ing can be expressed as the ice fraction s, whereas the
effective area is 1- s.

(3) The components of the reactive substance in the cell
and the ﬂow channel are uniform, that is, the reactive
gas does not produce a pressure drop in the ﬂow
channel, and the temperature change does not affect
the gas quality. Thereby, the constant parameters,
namely, the speciﬁc heat at a constant pressure, and the
gas mass ﬂow rate, can be obtained, allowing the reac-
tion rate to be expressed in terms of the constant
reactant-gas concentration.
(4) There is no liquid water in the fuel cell below the
freezing point.
Thermal analysis of cold-start processes
In the MEA, the change rate of water is equal to the difference
between the water generation rate owing to the fuel cell re-
action and water transport through the boundary. When the
temperature of the fuel cell is below the freezing point, the
produced water may be in the form of ionomer, gas phase and
ice phase. And ice will appear if the gas phase is saturated. The
cold start of a fuel cell can be divided into the following four
stages through the water phase transition:
The ﬁrst stage: The fuel cell starts up, and the cathode CL
produces water that accumulates in the ionomer and gas
phase.
The second stage: Once the water vapor in the CL reaches
saturation, the water produced accumulates as ice in the CL
[14,27].
The third stage: If the temperature of the PEMFC reaches
the freezing point before the CL completely freezes, the ice
accumulated in the CL will begin to melt.
The fourth stage: When all the ice accumulated in the CL
has melted, the fuel cell temperature further rises to the
normal operating temperature.
The water produced by the reaction in the second stage
freezes into ice at sub-zero temperature and the increase in
the ice volume fraction leads to a decrease in the electro-
chemically active area. The decrease in conductivity and the
hindrance of gas transmission slow down the reaction rate,
reduce heat generation and accelerate the accumulation of
ice. Furthermore, as the ice volume fraction increases, the
latent heat required for melting ice also increases, leading to a
longer duration of the third stage. To solve the contradiction
between heat generation and ice volume, a method for con-
trolling the current density through the heat absorbed by MEM
and ice volume is proposed to raise the heat generation under
the same ice volume and makes the fuel cell start successfully
at a lower temperature.

Cold start modes
Based on the above model, this study designs the cold start
mode of the fuel cell and simulates eight different start con-
ditions. The ambient temperature in Cases 1e5 is 263 K, and in
Cases 6e8, it is at 253 K.
Cases 1 and 2 use constant current density of 400 A/m
2 and
500 A/m 2 throughout the cold start. In Case 3, the current
density increases linearly from 400 A/m 2 to 500 A/m 2 within
100s. Case 4 linearly reduces the current density from 500 A/
m2 to 400 A/m 2 within 100s. In Case 6, the current density
linearly rises from 900 A/m 2 to 1000 A/m2 within 100s. In Case
7, the current density decreases linearly from 1000 A/m2 to 900
A/m2 within 100s. In Cases 5 and 8, the current variation is
affected by the ice volume and heat absorbed by the MEM
during the cold start. This relationship is explained in the next
section.
Based on the previous analysis, a new start-up mode is
designed to improve the operation of the fuel cell in low-
temperature environment. The control scheme uses ice vol-
ume, heat absorbed by the membrane and reference current
density as input, and outputs the corrected current density.
Owing to the large inertia and nonlinearity of the fuel cell, it is
very difﬁcult to achieve the desired control effect using a
conventional control strategy. Fuzzy control does not depend
on the system model and is suitable for nonlinear systems.
Therefore, a fuzzy control strategy for the cold start mode is
developed. Considering the system response delay, the output
current of the fuzzy control returns to the input of the fuel cell
model after passing through the ﬁrst-order inertia link.
In Cases 5 and 8, a fuzzy controller [35] is adopted to change
the output current according to the ice volume fraction and
heat absorbed by the MEM. More detailed information
regarding the fuzzy controller is shown in Fig. 3. It consists of
three main parts [ 36]:
1. The fuzziﬁcation interface uses fuzzy sets to convert spe-
ciﬁc inputs into fuzzy values.
2. The rule base and inference system generate a result for
each appropriate rule, and then merge these results.
3. The defuzziﬁcation interface converts the combined result
into a speciﬁc control output value.

The voltage in Cases 2 and 5 are essentially stable at
approximately 0.68 V during the ice accumulation period,
whereas the voltages of Cases 3 and 4 increase or decrease
between 0.64 V and 0.84 V as the current varies. In Cases 1 and
4, the voltages rise slowly at the beginning owing to the effect
of membrane hydration. The voltages go lower during the
middle period, reﬂecting the change in ohmic resistance
caused by the electro-osmotic drag effect and temperature
increase. At 262s and 271s, the ice volume fractions in Cases 1
and 4 reach 1, and the voltages drop sharply from 0.8 V to 0 V
because the ice fully covers the CL. At 194s, 210s and 168.4s,
the maximum ice volume fraction is reached in Cases 2, 3, and
5, and the corresponding minimum voltages are 0.65 V, 0.64 V
and 0.67 V. Subsequently, the voltages of Cases 2, 3 and 5
gradually increase to 0.72 V with ice melting. Compared with
the time required for the voltage to stabilize in Cases 2 and 3,
Case 5 has the fastest cold start.
When the MEM temperature rises to 273.15 K, the
maximum ice volume fractions in Cases 2, 3 and 5 reach 0.85,
0.88 and 0.60, respectively. In Cases 1 and 4, the maximum
MEM temperatures are 271.61 K and 271.76 K because of the
increase in the ice volume fraction to 1. Interestingly, when
the temperature rises to the freezing point or the ice volume
reaches 1, the maximum difference in heat generation is only
274 J, of which the lowest value is 2212.61 J in Case 5, and the
highest is 2485.29 J in Case 4. The main reason is that when the
current density in Case 4 goes down, the heat generation de-
creases, which is not enough to meet the melting heat corre-
sponding to ice generation. And the low temperature rise rate
extends the cold start time and leads to the increase of the
heat generation required for unit temperature rise. In view of
this, the heat absorption curve of the MEM and the curve of
heat transfer to the GDL are provided for further study.
It can be seen from Fig. 6 that when the heat absorption
curve is above the diagonal, the MEM absorbs sufﬁcient heat
before the ice volume fraction reaches 1 to raise the temper-
ature to the freezing point, indicating a successful cold start.
When the heat absorption curve is below the diagonal, the
heat absorbed by the MEM is not enough to raise the tem-
perature to the freezing point, indicating a failed cold start.
From the curves for Cases 2 and 5, it can be seen that the MEM
heat for rising from 263 K to the freezing point is approxi-
mately 332.98 J, which is closely related to the MEM mass and
thermal physical parameters of MEM, as well as ambient
temperature. The heat absorbed by the GDL in Case 5 is
1879.63 J and that absorbed by the GDL in Case 2 is 2132.99 J.
This large difference may be due to the slow rise of MEM
temperature in Case 2, resulting in a longer cold start time,
and inducing the MEM to transfer more heat to the GDL. In
Cases 1 and 4, the heat absorbed by the MEM are only 282.46 J
and 287.64 J, whereas the heat absorbed by the GDL are
2118.62 J and 2197.66 J. It can be seen that in Cases 1 and 4, the
heat absorbed by the MEM is lower than that in the other three
cases, but the heat transferred by the MEM is higher. This also
explains the failure of the cold start in Cases 1 and 4 when the
MEM heat generation in these ﬁve cases is similar. Therefore,
making the MEM absorb more heat and reduce heat transfer at

Conclusion
In this research, a mathematical model is established to
simulate the cold start process of the fuel cell, including the
calculation of the heat generation of the fuel cell and the
mutual heat transfer of its internal structure. Then, we
designed a cold start mode in which the ice volume and heat
absorbed by the MEM are the fuzzy control input, and current
density is the output. This mode is compared with cold-start
modes involving constant current and linear current change.
The main ﬁndings of this study are as follows:
(1) The proposed mode can control the current, so that the
heat exchange in the early stage is reduced, and the
MEM heat absorption rate in the later stage is main-
tained. As indicated by the temperature distribution
diagram, the heat absorbed by the MEM in the proposed
mode is approximately 20% e33% higher than that in
other modes with the same ice volume fraction (s ¼ 0.8)
at 253 K; furthermore, the outer layers have the lowest
temperature in the proposed mode with the same heat
absorbed by the MEM (282 J) at 263 K.
(2) At different ambient temperatures, the proposed mode
has the best cold start performance. Compared with
other modes at 263 K, the cold startup time is shortened
by 25.6se41.6s and the ice volume fraction is reduced by
29.4e31.8%. Furthermore, only the proposed mode
achieves a successful cold start at 253 K.
(3) Under the same electric quantity, the mode in which
the current gradually increases facilitates the cold start.
At 263 K, the start mode from low to high current ach-
ieves a successful cold start at 210s, whereas the start
mode from high to low current fails.
(4) The inertia link in the controller can adjust the ampli-
tude and rate of change of the current density during
cold start. The simulation results show that a larger
time constant can avoid the short-term lack of
hydrogen and oxygen during current loading: The cur-
