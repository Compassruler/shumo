abstract
To investigate cold start strategies at the system level, an integrated transient system model is developed,
consisting of stack, membrane humidi ﬁer, electrochemical hydrogen pump, compressor, and radiator.
The unassisted startup from /C0 10 /C14 C succeeds while it fails when started from /C0 20 /C14 C. To achieve the
successful startup from /C0 20 /C14 C, various assisted strategies are adopted. For reactant gas heating method,
the additional heat carried by gases is averaged about 1.2 W for each individual cell when the temper-
ature of humidi ﬁer and hydrogen pump are maintained at 60
/C14 C. Meanwhile, a large amount of moisture
is introduced to the stack, which may lead to accelerated failure. For stack heating method, the startup
succeeds if the total heating power reaches 40 W. However, the corresponding temperature difference
within stack reaches as large as 22.0
/C14 C, which indicates that improving the thermal conductivity of fuel
cell materials is of great importance. Under coolant heating method, the startup succeeds if the coolant
temperature reaches /C0 5 /C14 C, and the ice formation can even be avoided if the coolant temperature is kept
at 10 /C14 C. However, the power consumption for heating coolant is extremely large, indicating that sec-
ondary power sources are necessary.

1. Introduction
Owing to the advantages of high power density, high energy
conversion ef ﬁciency, and zero emissions, proton exchange mem-
brane fuel cell (PEMFC) is considered as one of the most important
power sources for the automobile transportation industry in the
prospective hydrogen society [ 1e3]. Several giant automobile
companies have already made fuel cell vehicles available to the
public, such as Mirai from Toyota [ 4], Clarity from Honda [ 5], and
Nexo from Hyundai [ 6]. Note that the powertrain technologies in
Clarity and Nexo have been both rated as Wards ten best engines
[7]. The application of hybrid fuel cell systems in unmanned aerial
vehicle [ 8], forklift [ 9], and ship [ 10] has also been reported.
However, there still remain some technical challenges before the
large-scale commercialization, among which the successful and
rapid startup from subzero temperatures is of great importance
[11]. Based on the ultimate targets of 80-kW integrated
transportation fuel cell power systems proposed by the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE), unassisted startup from /C0 30 /C14 C and
assisted startup from /C0 40 /C14 C are required [ 12]. Less than 30 s is
required for the fuel cell power system to achieve 50% rated power
when started from /C0 20 /C14 C[ 12]. As regards the startup/shutdown
durability, the output voltage deterioration after 5000 cycles is
required to be less than 5% under speci ﬁc testing protocols [ 12].
Despite the fact that many cold start researches have been con-
ducted, the aforementioned technical targets are still hard to be
achieved, which requires further investigations [ 11].
During cold start processes, the generated water through
hydrogen-oxygen reactions is likely to freeze if the local tempera-
ture is lower than the freezing point. Since ice is solid, it is not able
to move out of the porous layers, thus blocking effective reaction
sites in the catalyst layer (CL) and species transport pathways in the
gas diffusion layer (GDL) [ 13]. Once the air ﬂowing into the fuel cell
is unable to reach three-phase boundaries (TPB) in the CL or the
amount of oxygen that successfully reaches TPB is unable to
compensate for the electrochemical reaction consumption, the
startup is considered failed. From the perspective of thermal
behavior, the cold start can be generalized as the competition* Corresponding author.

visualization technologies been greatly developed, more researches

such as transparent fuel cell [ 33], infrared image [ 34], Raman

models have been developed to explain the coupled heat and mass
transport phenomena during sub-zero temperatures, including
lumped thermal model [ 39], analytical model [ 40], one-
dimensional [ 41e44], two-dimensional [ 45,46], and three-
dimensional models [ 47e49]. The application of arti ﬁcial intelli-
gence for numerical models [ 50e52] and optimization algorithms
for model parameter estimation [ 53,54] have also been presented.

From the perspective of fuel cell structural designs, the addition of
micro-porous layer was widely considered to be beneﬁcial [55e57].
As for preconditioning methods, the gas purge process after shut-
down was necessary because it minimized the amount of residual
water [25,26,58]. For startup operating conditions, various startup
modes and strategies were proposed such as maximum power
startup mode [ 59], variable heating and load control [ 60], and
model-based real-time adaptive control [ 61]. Besides, adopting the
methanol-water solution as antifreeze agent was found to inhibit
performance degradation after freezing\thawing cycles [ 62]. Note
that the above strategies were focused on the self-startup of single
cell or stack, requiring no additional heating power consumption
but relatively long warm-up time. Consequently, assisted cold start
strategies such as reactant gas heating [ 63], stack heating [ 64],
coolant heating [ 65], hydrogen-oxygen catalytic reaction [ 66,67],
and external hydrogen burner [68] were proposed to accelerate the
startup process. However, a comprehensive and quantitative
comparison among those assisted startup strategies, which
considered the operability, timeliness, ef ﬁciency, power con-
sumption, and local parameter distribution inside individual fuel
cells (such as current density, ice, temperature), was few to ﬁnd in
literature. Besides, the majority of aforementioned cold start re-
searches were concentrated on characterizing and optimizing the
startup capacity of single fuel cells or stacks, which hardly
addressed the problem at a comprehensive PEMFC system level. As
presented previously [ 69,70], a typical PEMFC system consisted of
stack, reactant gas supply subsystems, humidi ﬁcation subsystem,
and heat management subsystem. The hydrogen supply subsystem
consists of high-pressure hydrogen tank, pressure-regulating valve,
three-way valve, water knockout, and hydrogen recirculation
pump. The air supply subsystem includes air ﬁlter, compressor,
radiator, membrane humidi ﬁer, water knockout, and solenoid
valve. The heat management subsystem is composed of water tank,
pump, heater, thermostat, ion collector, and radiator. The stable
operation of system was jointly guaranteed by the coordination
among all subsystems while the couple water and heat manage-
ment within the whole system was extremely sophisticated [69,71].
Take the water transport for an example, the humidi ﬁcation of
membrane humidiﬁer relied on the stack cathode exhausted gases
while it had great in ﬂuences on stack performances during cold
start processes. The successful systematic startup from subzero
temperatures should not only raise the stack temperature over the
freezing point rapidly but also simultaneously accelerate the tem-
perature increment of auxiliary components without suf ﬁcient in-
ternal heat generation (such as membrane humidi ﬁer). However,
few related researches were presented in literature, making it
necessary to investigate the cold start issue from the view of an
authentic system level.
In the study, an integrated transient PEMFC system model that
has been developed in our previous studies [ 69,70] is adopted to
investigate the cold start capability of whole system. The unassisted
systematic cold start performances under different startup oper-
ating conditions are brie ﬂy investigated, including the distribution
of output voltage, ice volume fraction, and temperature. To achieve
the successful startup from subzero temperatures as low as /C0 20 /C14 C,
assisted startup strategies such as reactant gas heating method,
stack heating method, and coolant heating method are quantita-
tively compared. The output performance, stack inlet reactant
relative humidity, ice generation rate, temperature distribution,
and cold start duration are comprehensively investigated. The
effectiveness of assisted startup strategies is evaluated, which gives
valuable suggestions on cold start issues.
2. Model development
Methodologies about the core sub-models are brie ﬂy presented,
including stack, membrane humidi ﬁer, electrochemical hydrogen
pump, compressor, and radiator. The integration of various cold
start strategies and the system model is also presented.
2.1. Stack model
Proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) typically consists
of bipolar plate (BP), gas channel, gas diffusion layer (GDL), micro-
porous layer (MPL), catalyst layer (CL), and proton exchange
membrane. For the stack composed of many single cells, the
amount of reactant gases and coolant ﬂowing into each individual
fuel cell are typically different owing to pressure losses during the
motion of ﬂuids [ 72,73]. However, it is found that the ﬂow distri-
bution uniformity can be signi ﬁcantly improved through the opti-
mization of stack manifold geometry in our recent study [ 74].
Besides, low current densities (such as 0.1 A cm /C0 2) are widely
adopted in cold start operations, which reduces the distribution
non-uniformity to a large extent because the mass ﬂow rate of
reactants is relatively small. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume
that reactant gases and coolant are uniformly distributed into every
fuel cell in the presented study. In addition, mass transport pro-
cesses in porous layers are assumed to be diffusion-dominated in
the through-plane direction [ 75,76]. The product water is assumed
to be membrane water initially according to the previous study [44]
in which the phase transition mechanisms during cold start pro-
cesses have been comprehensively investigated. Phase changes
among water vapor, membrane water, super-cooled/liquid water,
and ice are fully taken into consideration. Heat radiation between
the stack and surroundings is neglected while heat convection is
taken into account. The stack structural properties and operating
conditions are listed in Table 1. The governing equations of mem-
brane water, super-cooled/liquid water, gas species, ice, tempera-
ture, and output voltage are summarized in Table 2 while the
source terms are summarized in Table 3 . Note that the heat and
mass transfer processes are calculated in the through-plane direc-
tion as well as the gas channel direction. Therefore, the parameter
variations from upstream to downstream can be clearly demon-
strated, including local current density, ice volume fraction, tem-
perature, and other key parameters [ 69,70]. Besides, performance
differences (such as output voltage and temperature) among
various single fuel cells can also be well demonstrated. The detailed
correlations for electrochemical and transport properties have been
presented in the previous studies [ 43,69].

authentic freezing temperature in porous layers is lower than the
normal freezing point ( TN ¼ 273.15 K), which is called the freezing

2.2. Membrane humidi ﬁer model
To utilize stack cathode exhausted gases, a planar membrane
humidiﬁer is adopted, which absorbs the moisture and conse-
quentially humidiﬁes the dry air. The membrane humidi ﬁer model
mainly consists of gas channels and membrane. The addition of
obstacles in ﬂow ﬁeld and GDL to enhance the diffusion of water
vapor transfer has also been reported [ 77,78]. The ice formation
inside membrane humidi ﬁer is neglected because the amount of
super-cooled water along with stack cathode exhausted gases is
tiny. It should be mentioned that there is no suf ﬁcient internal heat
generation inside the membrane humidi ﬁer, and the operating
temperature is likely to be much lower than that of stack [ 69].
Under the circumstances, water vapor condensation is possible to
happen in the humidi ﬁer wet channel, but the quantity is minor,
which has negligible in ﬂuences on humidi ﬁer performances.
Therefore, the amount of internally generated super-cooled water
is also neglected in the cold start operation. Governing equations of
membrane water, water vapor, and temperature as well as corre-
sponding source terms are given in Table 4.
2.3. Electrochemical hydrogen pump model
A hydrogen recirculation pump is placed in the hydrogen supply
subsystem to partially recycle the used hydrogen, enhancing fuel
utilization and anode reactant humidiﬁcation [79,80]. The structure
of electrochemical hydrogen pump is similar to that of PEMFC, and
the corresponding structural properties of electrochemical pump
are set the same as that of PEMFC. Detailed information of working
principles and transport properties has been presented in our
previous study [ 69,70], and the governing equations are listed in
Table 5.

2.5. Radiator model

dissipate the heat carried by coolant to surroundings. The ribbon-

tween coolant and surroundings can be divided into three stages,
which includes heat convection between coolant and tube, heat
conduction inside tube wall, and heat convection between tube and
air. Under steady-state conditions, the released heat from coolant is
equal to the absorbed heat by air.

As for the integration of assisted startup strategies with the
system model, different methodologies are adopted. At the system

midiﬁer while the anode inlet temperature is calculated as the
mixed temperature between recycled gases and pure hydrogen.

the temperature of membrane humidi ﬁer and electrochemical
hydrogen pump. The stack heating method is typically achieved by
installing heating wires at the two sides of stack, thus directly
raising the stack temperature by Joule heat. In the system model, a
speciﬁc heat source is added to the two end plates. As for the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Model validation
To ensure the reliability of integrated proton exchange mem-
brane fuel cell (PEMFC) system model, all sub-models have been
validated against experimental data. For the stack sub-model, grid
independence study, polarization curve at different cathode inlet
relative humidity, and output voltage under failed/successful cold
start conditions have been presented in the previous study [ 70]. In
addition, the model has also been compared with our latest
experimental data [ 82].
Fig. 2 shows the output voltage and the ohmic voltage when
started from /C0 15 /C14 C and /C0 20 /C14 C. The current density is set as
0.1 A cm /C0 2. Special attention should be paid to the beginning of
corresponding experimental data in Ref. [ 82] because there is no
current load in the ﬁrst 10 s. Therefore, the actual and effective
experimental data of startup processes is selected after the startup
duration reaches 10 s, which is used to further validate the stack
sub-model. Good agreement between our laboratory experiments
and simulation results is observed. As for the four auxiliary sub-
models, detailed model validation can also be found in the previ-
ous study [ 70].
3.2. Unassisted startup performances
Based on the previous study [ 70], the co-current ﬂow conﬁgu-
ration contributes to water vapor accumulation in the gas channel
direction inside fuel cells, leading to higher water utilization of the
overall system. Therefore, it is unadvisable to adopt the co- ﬂow
pattern since more ice is likely to be formed in the stack
compared with the counter-current ﬂow pattern. Consequently, the
stack is placed in counter- ﬂow con ﬁguration in the presented re-
sults. The unassisted startup performances are ﬁrstly investigated,
which provide a benchmark against the following assisted startup
strategies. The stack output voltage under the ambient temperature
of /C0 10 /C14 C and /C0 20 /C14 C is shown in Fig. 3 . The constant current
density mode is adopted because it results in a relatively stable
water generation rate, which contributes to the stack operation
stability. The stack is successful to reach the freezing point tem-
perature when started from /C0 10 /C14 C while it fails when started
from /C0 20 /C14 C. For the successful cases, a higher operating current
density largely accelerates the startup speed owing to the higher
amount of generated heat. In the failed cases, the startup duration
is around 80 s at 0.1 A cm /C0 2 while it only lasts for about 25 s with
the current density of 0.2 A cm /C0 2. Since the stack is initially dry and
the electro-osmotic drag effect becomes more signi ﬁcant with
growing current densities, the membrane water content gradient
between anode and cathode increases at 0.2 A cm /C0 2, leading to
higher ohmic voltage losses and sharper output voltage drops in
the beginning. Therefore, increasing the current density does not
necessarily lead to enhanced cold start survivability because more
water is simultaneously generated, which is likely to expedite the
ice formation inside porous layers.
The distribution of ice volume fraction in cathode catalyst layer
(CL) along the channel direction inside a single fuel cell is shown in
Fig. 4(a), and the ice distribution among different fuel cells is shown

there exist obvious ice volume fraction differences from the reac-

ice is formed at the same startup time. The ice volume fraction in
node 1 grows faster than that of node 2 and node 3. The main
reason for above non-uniform ice formation rates is the variations
of local current density, membrane water content, and temperature

3.5. Coolant heating method
Since coolant is able to absorb heat from the stack under normal
operating temperatures, it is also possible for coolant to heat the
stack under cold start conditions. By placing a heater in the heat
management subsystem, the coolant temperature can be largely
increased before ﬂowing into the stack. Fig. 1 1 shows the stack
voltage under different coolant inlet temperatures. It is found that
the stack voltage improves with higher coolant temperatures,
which results from the enhanced fuel cell operating temperature.
Although the stack still fails when the coolant temperature
is /C0 10
/C14 C, the cold start process succeeds when the temperature
reaches /C0 5 /C14 C. In addition, the startup duration is reduced to about
72.8 s if the coolant temperature further rises to 0 /C14 C. Table 7 lists
the maximum ice volume fraction in cathode CL, maximum tem-
perature difference, and cold start duration with different coolant
inlet temperatures from /C0 10 /C14 Ct o2 0 /C14 C. Small temperature dif-
ferences are observed, indicating that the temperature distribution
is relatively uniform inside the whole stack. Unlike the stack
heating method, the additional heat carried by coolant is evenly
distributed into each fuel cells, which raises the stack temperature
uniformly and rapidly. When the coolant inlet temperature is kept
at 10 /C14 C, the ice formation can even be avoided, which is of great
importance for cold start strategy optimizations. However, it should
be noted that the power consumption for heating coolant is
extremely large. Take the coolant composed of 50% glycol and 50%
water for an example, the power requirement to raise 0.1 kg s /C0 1
coolant from /C0 20 /C14 Ct o0 /C14 C is calculated to be around 6.2 kW.
Therefore, it is unrealistic to merely rely on the stack output power
to satisfy the power consumption, implying that secondary power
sources are necessary for the coolant heating method.
The ice volume fraction in cathode CL of fuel cell 1 is shown in
Fig. 12 (a), and the corresponding CL temperature is shown in
Fig. 12(b). The node 3 is selected because it represents the center
region of fuel cell in channel direction. It is observed that the ice
generation rate changes in the case of /C0 10 /C14 C since the curve slope
is not constant. Before around 60 s, the slope keeps decreasing
slightly, and it is smaller than that of the unassisted case. Subse-
quently, the slope increases insigniﬁcantly, which indicates that the
ice formation grows faster. In the case of /C0 5 /C14 C, the curve slope also
changes throughout the whole process. As shown in Fig. 12(b), the
CL temperature indeed increases much faster with the coolant
heating method in comparison with the unassisted case. Note that
the temperature difference between coolant and stack is large in
the beginning, which correspondingly results in the high temper-
ature increase rate. With the CL temperature rising, the curve slope
drops, indicating a decreased temperature growing rate. As stated
in the phase change mechanisms, the super-cooled water is
generated if the membrane water gets saturated while the satu-
rated membrane water content is dependent on the local temper-
ature. The heat absorbed from the coolant accelerates the stack
temperature increment, which decreases the difference between
the membrane water content and the saturated content, leading to
lower ice formation rates. Furthermore, if the membrane hydration
rate through electrochemical reactions is hard to catch the growing
rate of saturated membrane water content, the ice formation will be
strongly suppressed.
4. Conclusions
In the study, an integrated proton exchange membrane fuel cell
system model, which is composed of stack, membrane humidi ﬁer,
electrochemical hydrogen pump, compressor, and radiator, is
developed to investigate the cold start performances. The integra-
tion of all sub-models has been presented in detail. All sub-models
have been validated against experimental data, especially the
pseudo two-dimensional stack sub-model. The unassisted cold
start performances are ﬁrstly demonstrated. The stack is able to
reach the freezing point temperature when started from /C0 10
/C14 C
while it fails when started from /C0 20 /C14 C. It is further observed that
the ice volume fraction of cathode catalyst layer varies in the gas
channel direction inside a single fuel cell. However, there exist no
remarkable output voltage variations and temperature variations
among individual fuel cells.
