abstract
The water in polymer electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs) operated below the freezing point in-
terferes with the gas supply, shutting down the operation. Therefore, understanding the
freezing mechanism is pivotal to achieving high-performance cold-start. In this paper, a
novel method of classifying two different freezing modes using 6 mm thickness thin-ﬁlm
thermocouples (TFTCs) was proposed. We conducted in situ measurement of the solidiﬁ-
cation heat from supercooled water using originally developed TFTCs and further visual-
ized the ice distribution using X-ray CT. In a mode when TFTCs measure rapid temperature
rises in the /C0 10
/C14 C~ /C0 5 /C14 C, water freezes after diffusing into the GDL, while freezing occurs
immediately inside the CL without reaching the GDL in the other mode when TFTCs
measure no rapid temperature rise in the /C0 30
/C14 C~ /C0 10 /C14 C. These modes should be
considered for future researches on components improvement, and optimization of cur-
rent density sweeping to succeed the cold start.

Introduction
Polymer electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs) are used as power
generating devices for commercial purposes, such as auto-
mobiles or household, owing to their high efﬁciency and
power output [ 1]. However, PEFCs face various technical
challenges, such as cold-start and moisture control [ 2]. In
particular, the voltage drop across the cell due to freezing of
water inside the cell is a serious hindrance in realizing high
performance in cold environments.
Previous studies have been reported to understand the
mechanisms that occur at the cold-start of a cell, achieving a
fair degree of success with high-performance cold-starting of
PEFCs. Kim et al. [ 3,4] investigated the effect of a heating and
cooling cycle, in the temperature range of /C0 40e70
/C14 C, on the
components of a PEFC. They reported that the ice formed in-
side the CL of the PEFC broke the CL structure. Furthermore,
the ice formed at the interface of the components created a
barrier that separated the components, and consequently
increased the proton transport and the ohmic resistance
across them. However, these reports are only ex situ mea-
surements and do not consider the effects on the actual
operation below the freezing point. Guo et al. [ 5] used cyclic
voltammetry to investigate the subfreezing physical proper-
ties and performance of a hydrated membrane electrode as-
sembly (MEA) undergoing a heating and cooling cycle. The
result revealed that the freezing had caused a decrease in the
electrochemical active area, but did not affect the short-term
performance of the cell. These investigations further reveal
that freezing causes irreversible damage to the cell
components.
Generally, water generated by the electrochemical reaction
exists as ice below the freezing point. However, at the cold-
start of a PEFC, the water exists in the liquid phase, i.e. the
supercooled state, for a certain period of time [ 6]. Therefore,
power generation does not terminate at the same time as the
startup even at 0
/C14 C, and it can be sustained until the super-
cooled water has ﬁlled the void in the CL. In addition, Tajiri
et al. proposed gas purging to remove the water from the CL
and gas diffusion layer (GDL) and decide the initial water
content of proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell changes
the power generation time [ 7]. The lesser the initial water
content, the lesser the open circuit voltage and the longer the
power generation time due to the securing of the PEM water
storage. Based on these results, Tajiri et al. [ 8] investigated the
process of water evaporation during the purging using an
electrochemical measurement method. However, these
studies do not specify the local temperature inside the cell.
In other studies, visualization of ice distribution inside the
cell has been performed to understand the effect of ice and
supercooled water on the performance of the fuel cell. For this
visualization, X-ray computed tomography (CT) [ 9e18], mag-
netic resonance imaging (MRI) [ 19e21], and neutron radiog-
raphy [ 22e25] under a normal operating temperature range
have been used. However, to conduct the visualization at
freezing point, a system to cool the cell to subzero tempera-
tures is required. Because introducing cooling systems into
these devices is difﬁcult, there have been few reports on the in
situ visualization at the freezing point. Moreover, in general,
visualizing near the CL with a CT device is difﬁcult because the
amount of Pt X-ray absorption is too large. Furthermore, the
spatial resolution of neutron radiography is so low that it is
difﬁcult to observe the ﬁne ice distribution. Therefore, post-
analysis methods, such as CRYO-SEM, to inspect the in-
teriors of the cell after shutdown while maintaining subzero
temperatures [ 26e28] or visualization methods [ 29e31] using
transparent cells are often used. For instance, Tabe et al.
visualized ice distribution by observing the cell after shut-
down using CRYO-SEM [ 28]. The result revealed that the
freezing mechanism varies with the temperature. It was re-
ported that the product water freezes near the PEM eCL
interface at /C0 20
/C14 C and freezes around the GDL at /C0 10 /C14 C
[32]. Moreover, Ishikawa et al. [ 30], using a transparent cell,
observed that the generated water deposited on the CL surface
had maintained its supercooled state for a certain period of
time. This subcooled water freezes when the radius of the
water droplets exceeds a speciﬁc value, and both the numer-
ical analysis and the experiments demonstrated that this
value strongly depended on the degree of subcooling [ 31].
Furthermore, Oberholzer et al. used neutron imaging to
visualize the in-plane directional water distribution and
showed that the generated water exists as supercooled water
in the range of /C0 20
/C14 Ce/C0 10 /C14 C[ 33]. In addition, they reported
the shutdown to have occurred stochastically.
Other researchers investigated the effect of components on
the performance of the fuel cell at cold-start. For example, Du
et al. studied the effect of the microporous layer (MPL) on the
cell performance, and demonstrated the decrease in the hu-
midity dependence of the cell performance and the
improvement of cell performance by an anode MPL [ 34].
Watanabe et al. investigated the inﬂuence of GDL pore size on
performance, and reported that a GDL with a larger pore size
increased the diameter of the ice formation and made it easier
for ﬂooding to occur due to the disturbance of the gas by the
large ice [ 35]. Moreover, they presented a new GDL with a
three-layer-structuredMPL, carbon paper, and hydrophilic
layer. The hydrophilic layer expels the water in the cell,
improving the cell performance [ 36].
Additionally, Ju et al. [ 37,38] developed a multidimensional
transient cold start model to predict cold start behavior and
proposed an efﬁcient cold start-up strategy that involves
raising the operating current of the PEFC numerically.
Furthermore, they showed the effects of CL design parameters
on the cold start behavior and provided a novel MPL to
enhance the cold start capability of PEFCs using cold start
model [39,40].
As mentioned above, research into the cold-start of PEFC
has been widely conducted, such as component deterioration
analysis, effect of water content of PEM, visualization of ice
distribution, component effect determination and cold start
model simulation. However, these reports are not sufﬁcient
because in situ measurements have not been conducted and
well-substantiated explanations for the freezing mechanism
have not been provided. To the best of our knowledge, no in
situ temperature measurement method on PEFC cold start
exists. Moreover, many experiments have used a cell other
than that of the actual devices.
In this study, we succeeded in measuring the local tem-
perature inside the cell during cold start and further

Materials and methods
Cell design and components of polymer electrolyte fuel cell
Fig. 1(a) illustrates the conﬁguration of the small PEFC fuel cell
used for this experiment. In addition, as depicted in Fig. 1 (b),
carbon separators (Special Carbon Co., Ltd.) with ﬁve parallel
channels were used. The width and depth of all the channels
and ribs of the cell were 1 mm, and the reaction area was
1c m
2. Additionally, a 30 mm-thick ﬂuorinated polymer elec-
trolyte membrane was applied on a catalyst-coated mem-
brane (CCM), to ensure water absorption in the proton-
exchange membrane (PEM) at cold start. SGL28BC (SIGRA-
CET) was applied on a hydrophobic gas diffusion layer (GDL).
Moreover, cell compression was adjusted by varying the
thickness of the black gasket. The optimal gasket thickness
was selected by subjecting a GDL to the dry compression test
and measuring its thickness after the loading compression
using an X-ray computed tomography (CT) device. As a result,
a 180 mm-thick gasket was selected, so that the cell
compression would be approximately 1 MPa.
Thin-ﬁlm thermocouple (TFTC)
In this experiment, a ﬂexible thin-ﬁlm thermocouple (TFTC)
with good mechanical strength was fabricated using micro
electromechanical systems (MEMS) technology and inserted
into the fuel cell to perform in situ measurement of the cell at
cold-start. As the thickness of the components of PEFC was
approximately 10 e100 mm, wafer-thin thermocouples were
used for the direct measurement of the cell without affecting
the internal reactions [ 41]. A schematic of the TFTC is shown
in Fig. 2 . The electrodes of the TFTC comprised Ni and Cr.
Additionally, a layer of Parylene C, a type of polymer material,
was used to insulate the TFTC. The total length, thickness
including the insulation layer, and minimum width of the
measured part of the sensor were approximately 66 mm, 6 mm,
and 30 mm, respectively.

The cell temperature was measured using a K-type ther-

experiments. In addition, the current density was constantly

2 using an electronic load device

temperature were recorded using a data logger (Yokogawa
Electric, MX-100).
X-ray CT visualization
X-ray CT device (Yamato, TDM1000H-II) was used to visualize
ice distribution in the cell after shutdown occurred. This CT
device was also used in our resent research [ 41e43]. The X-ray
tube voltage was set at 30 kV, the tube current was 100 mA,
whole scanning time was 15 min, and a pixel size was 6.4 mm.

Generally, the degree of subcooling at /C0 5 /C14 C is so minute
that the water produced inside the cell remains in the
supercooled state. In addition, because the exothermic reac-
tion occurs with solidiﬁcation, the cause of rapid temperature
rise at each interface and the cathode separator can be
considered as the heat generated when the supercooled water
freezes. To conﬁrm this, an experiment for solidiﬁcation heat
measurement was conducted without using PEFC, as depicted
in Fig. 5 (a). First, a TFTC was placed on the stage and water
was poured on its temperature measurement area. Next, the
stage was gradually cooled using cooling water. Fig. 5 (b) de-
picts the temperature change of a droplet as indicated by the
TFTC at the instant the supercooled droplet freezes. In this
measurement, the solidiﬁcation heat was observed as an
instantaneous temperature change in 1 s. Because this
behavior is similar to the instantaneous temperature rise
inside the cell during cold-start, the momentary temperature
rise of each cathode side interface can be considered as a
result of the freezing of water.
The temperature of each interface and separator increased
almost simultaneously with the rapid cell voltage drop. The
reason for this voltage drop could be that the ice blocked the
supply of reaction gas and caused ﬂooding. Furthermore,
because the temperature rise at the CL eMPL interface was the
fastest, the origin of the freezing phenomenon was inside the
CL or near the TFTC inserted in the CL eMPL interface.
Thereafter, the TFTC inserted into the GDL eSep. interface
exhibited a temperature rise. This implies that the TFTC in the
GDLeSep. interface measured the heat transfer from the ﬁrst
solidiﬁcation or the solidiﬁcation heat of the supercooled
water deposited in the cathode GDL due to heat transfer.

Conclusions
The PEFC cold start experiments were performed while
measuring the temperature of cathode CL/MPL interface and
GDL/Separator interface. The operation temperatures were/C0 5,
/C0 10, /C0 20, and /C0 30 /C14 C. As a result, the power generating dura-
tion tends to be shorter due to the lower operating temperature
since the generated water tends to solidify, and the ice causes
ﬂooding and forcibly terminates the operation as the degree of
supercooling increases. However, at subzero temperatures
higher than /C0 10
/C14 C, it is easier for water to exist in a super-
cooled state, which leads to the higher dependence of the
probability of freezing of supercooled water on shutdown.
Additionally, the sharp temperature rise at the CL eMPL inter-
face and the GDL eSep. interface increases with the operating
temperature due to the increase in the amount of supercooled
water in the cell with the power generating duration.
There are two different freezing modes: the rapid tem-
perature rise mode and the no rapid temperature rise mode
during PEFC cold start. In the rapid temperature rise mode in
the /C0 10
/C14 C~ /C0 5 /C14 C, the water generated by the electrochemical
reaction remained in the supercooled state, and froze after it
reached the GDL or separator. On the other hand, in the no
rapid temperature rise mode in the /C0 30
/C14 C~ /C0 10 /C14 C, water
froze immediately after the start of the operation inside the CL
without reaching the GDL.
Distinguishing these two freezing modes is signiﬁcant to
optimize the properties of components, such as GDL and MPL,
and improve the current density sweep method to succeed the
cold start. In this paper, the solidiﬁcation heat from super-
cooled water freezing during the PEFC cold-start was measured
using originally developed 6 mm thickness thin-ﬁlm thermo-
couples (TFTCs) and the visualization of ice distribution using
X-ray CT was conducted. Results show that the in situ mea-
surement of solidiﬁcation heat using TFTCs can be a novel and
simple method to classify two freezing modes during cold start.
