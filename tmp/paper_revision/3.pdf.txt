abstract
Startup from subzero temperatures, referred to as “cold start”, has been one of the technical challenges
hindering the commercialization of polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cell (PEMFC). In this study, a
mathematical model has been developed to simulate the cold start processes in a PEMFC. The present
three-dimensional multiphase model uniquely includes the water freezing in the membrane electrolyte,
the non-equilibrium mass transfer between the water in the ionomer and the water (vapour, liquid and
ice) in the pore region of the catalyst layer (CL), and the water freezing and melting in the CL and gas dif-
fusion layer (GDL). Both the failed and successful cold start processes have been numerically investigated.
Numerical results indicate that increasing the ionomer fraction in the cathode CL has more signiﬁcant
effects than increasing the thickness of the membrane layer in reducing the amount of ice formation, and
the ohmic heat is the largest heating source at low cell voltages. It is observed that water freezes ﬁrst in
the cathode CL under the land, and ice melts ﬁrst in the CLs under the ﬂow channel, the melted water in
the anode is also removed faster than in the cathode.

1. Introduction
Low operating temperature, high power density and zero/low
emission have made polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cell
(PEMFC) the most promising fuel cell type for the future in auto-
motive applications. It appears to offer the best prospect for
further improvement until commercialization. Over past decades
PEMFC technology has achieved signiﬁcant improvements in terms
of performance, durability, stability and cost. However, further
improvement is necessary before commercial application, such as
cold start in winter. In winter conditions, it is unavoidable for vehi-
cles driving below the freezing point of water (0
◦C), therefore,
for successful commercialization of PEMFC in automotive applica-
tions, rapid startup from subzero temperatures must be achieved,
which is referred to as “cold start” of PEMFC. The major problem
of PEMFC cold start is that the product water freezes when the
temperature inside the PEMFC is lower than the freezing point
of water. If the catalyst layer (CL) is fully covered by ice before
the cell temperature rises above freezing point, the electrochem-
ical reaction may be stopped due to the blockage of the reaction
site. In addition, ice formation may also result in serious damage to
the structure of the membrane electrode assembly (MEA). There-
fore, for PEMFC in automotive applications, successful cold start is

of paramount importance. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
already established a challenging technical target: rapid startup
of PEMFC to 90% rated power from −20
◦C for 30 s in 2010 [1].
At present, rapid unassisted cold start of PEMFC is still very dif-
ﬁcult. Most of the present PEMFCs employ various assisted cold
start methods such as: resistance heating using DC from batteries,
coolant heating, hot air blowing, catalytic hydrogen/oxygen reac-
tion inside the PEMFC, etc. United Technology Corporation (UTC)
achieved 34% rated power in 30 s from −30
◦C, they also achieved
unassisted cold start from −35◦C, but the duration is not reported
[2]. Ballard Mark1100 PEMFC power module also has cold start
capability from −15◦C [3]. Apparently PEMFC cold start capability
still needs signiﬁcant improvement, especially for unassisted cold
start, this is because assisted cold start might increase the volume
and weight of the system, as well as the operation complexity and
installation costs. However, only few of the numerously previous
numerical and experimental PEMFC studies paid attention to cold
start, even more signiﬁcant effort is needed in this area for PEMFC
commercialization.
By comparing with the experimental studies focusing on normal
operating conditions, the cold start related experimental studies
are much less. Tajiri et al.[4,5] designed experimental procedures to
mainly investigate the effects of MEA characteristics on PEMFC cold
start, and the initial water content in Naﬁon membrane was con-
trolled by different purging methods. They found that the best cold
start capability is achieved with the lowest initial water content,
because more product water can be taken by the membrane, result-

the solution purge (purged with antifreezes such as methanol)

experimental studies that focused on the effects of purging meth-
ods [10], startup temperatures [11], and purging temperatures [12]
on cold start performance were also carried out. Thompson et
al. [13] experimentally determined the oxygen reduction reaction
(ORR) kinetics for PEMFC operating at subzero temperatures, and
no signiﬁcant change in the ORR mechanism was found. Ge and
Wang [14,15] conducted the ﬁrst two in situ visualizations of ice
formation on the cathode CL surface by cooling the transparent
cells with coolant recirculation, and they have reported that the
freezing point of water in the CL is around −1
◦C. Ishikawa et al.
[16] also reported that the freezing temperature of liquid water
in the CL changes between −0.1 and −2.2 ◦C. All these studies
[14–16] showed that liquid water freezes at the temperatures lower
than 0 ◦C, known as the freezing point depression (FPD). Sun et
al. [17] described a new method called catalytic hydrogen/oxygen
reaction assisted cold start by supplying mixed hydrogen and air
(or oxygen) into the fuel cell, however, further investigations are
needed for this method. McDonald et al. [18] conducted an ex situ
freeze–thaw study on MEA, and they reported that no degrada-
tion was observed for dry MEA. Cappadonia et al.[19,20]conducted
experimental measurements of Naﬁon membrane conductance at
various subzero temperatures, and Thompson et al. [21] reported
the conductivities of Naﬁon membrane at different subzero temper-
atures based on the measured membrane conductance and sample
size. The measurements in [19–21] all observed sharp decrements
of membrane conductance/conductivity at subzero temperatures.
In [21], it was also found that such sharp decrements occurred when
the water phase changes were observed, and the amount of non-
frozen membrane water content at different subzero temperatures
were also estimated.
Similar to the experimental studies, only a few numerical mod-
els for PEMFC cold start are available in literature. Sundaresan and
Moore [22] conducted an analytical model for cold start of PEMFC
stacks. This one-dimensional model could predict the temperature
for each of the single cell by performing energy balance and heat
transfer analysis, and this model could also reveal the effects of the
endplate thermal mass and the internal heating method for PEMFC
cold start. Khandelwal et al.[23] also conducted a one-dimensional
thermal model, and similar to Sundaresan and Moore[22], the cold
start capability of PEMFC stacks can be evaluated. They reported
that adjusting the startup current density, coolant heating, isolation
of stack endplates are all effective ways to optimize the cold start
performance. Mao and Wang [24] developed an analytical model,
not only for temperature, this one-dimensional model can also pre-
dict the amount of ice formation in CL, water transport, changes of
cell voltage and current density, etc. Wang [25] also conducted an
analytical study and deﬁned some important parameters affecting
PEMFC cold start performance. The analytical models in [22–25]
can only roughly predict the PEMFC cold start performance, and in
order to investigate the fundamental physics of PEMFC cold start,
multi-dimensional and multiphase models are needed. However,
very few literatures are related to this ﬁeld[26–29]. Ahluwalia and
Wang [26] conducted a simple two-dimensional cold start model
for single PEMFCs, and they reported that high startup current den-
sity is favorable for rapid cold start, and they also investigated the
effects of feed gas temperatures, operating pressure, and electri-
cal heating on cold start performance. Mao et al. [27] developed a
three-dimensional, multiphase model, Meng[28] conducted a two-
dimensional multiphase model for PEMFC cold start simulations,
and based on the work in Mao et al. [27], Jiang el al. [29] further
conducted non-isothermal cold start simulations for PEMFC. How-
ever, all the numerical models in [27–29] assumed instantaneous
desublimation of water vapour to ice, and the reaction product is
water vapour. Since no liquid water is considered, so that these
models can only provide reasonable results when the temperature
is lower than the freezing point of water.
Based on the literature review, it is found that development of
multi-dimensional, multiphase PEMFC cold start model with the
capability to consider the detailed phase changes of water in both
the membrane electrolyte and the pore volumes of the GDL and
CL needs to be carried out for better predictions of cold start per-

2. Cold start model
2.1. Conservation equations
Fig. 1 shows the computational domain and mesh for the cold
start simulations, it can be noticed that all the major components
of a PEMFC are considered in this model, which are the bipolar
plate (BP), ﬂow channel, GDL, CL and membrane. The cell properties
and operating conditions are listed in Table 1. Fourteen conser-
vation equations are developed and to be solved simultaneously
in different domains, as listed in Table 2. These equations include
the conservations of mass, momentum ( x, y and z velocities), gas
species (hydrogen, oxygen and water vapour), liquid water, ice, non-
frozen membrane water content, frozen membrane water content,
electronic potential, ionic potential, and energy. The corresponding
source terms of the 14 conservation equations are listed inTable 3.

2.3. Numerical procedures
The governing equations are discretized and solved in the com-
mercial CFD software package Fluent 6.3 based on ﬁnite volume
method. Equations and physical properties are written and imple-
mented by using its user-deﬁned function (UDF). Because cold start
simulations are time dependent, the PISO algorithm is used which
is based on predictor–corrector splitting for unsteady problems.
An algebraic multigrid (AMG) method with a Gauss–Seidel type
smoother is used to accelerate the convergence. Adaptive time
stepping is employed, and for the simulations presented in this
paper, a minimum of 10
−6 s and a maximum of 0.1 s time step sizes
are used. Strict convergence criteria with a residual of 10 −8 are
used for all variables. The total number of cell shown in Fig. 1 is
76,000, and structured mesh is used for the whole computational
domain. Each cell in the ﬂow channel and BP has the same size
with dimensions of 1.0 mm× 0.1 mm× 0.1 mm, along thex-, y- and
z-directions, respectively. The mesh size along the y-direction is
reduced to 0.0178, 0.001 and 0.02 mm for the membrane, CL and
GDL, respectively, to ensure at least 10 cells along the y-direction
in each layer. Grid independent study was carefully carried out.
The difference in results (variations of cell temperature, current
density, water content, ice formation) by further increasing the
number of cells (up to two times of the one used) is negligible.
Such meshing systems were also considered sufﬁcient and widely
used in previous three-dimensional multiphase simulations (for
both the subzero and normal operating temperatures) [27,29,30].

4. Conclusion
In this paper, a mathematical model has been developed to sim-
ulate the cold start processes in a PEMFC. Unique in the present
three-dimensional multiphase cold start model is the inclusion of
the following phenomena: (i) the water freezing in the membrane
electrolyte and its effects on the membrane conductivity; (ii) the
non-equilibrium mass transfer between the water in the ionomer
and the water (vapour, liquid and ice) in the pore region of the
catalyst layer (CL); and (iii) both the water freezing and melting
in the CL and gas diffusion layer (GDL). Good agreement between
the model predictions and published experimental data has been
obtained. Numerical simulations have been conducted for a sin-
gle PEMFC starting at different subzero temperatures ( −10
◦Cf o r
failed cold start and −3 ◦C for successful cold start). Two inlet gas
temperatures are considered, either equal to the cell temperatures
or the ambient temperatures, and no noticeable difference in cold
start performance between these two conditions are observed, due
to the fact that the extra heat carried by the inlet gases for the
case that the inlet gas temperatures equal to the cell temperatures
is much lower than the heat generation from the electrochemical
reaction. It is also found that increasing the startup current den-
sity can signiﬁcantly accelerate the cold start process. For the cold
start from −10
◦C (failed cold start), it is observed that the ice ﬁrst
appears under the land and at the locations away from both the
membrane/CL and GDL/CL interfaces. The simulation results also
show that the water production rate is higher than the water diffu-
sion rate in the ionomer (low diffusivity at subzero temperatures),
therefore increasing the ionomer fraction in the cathode CL has
more signiﬁcant effects than increasing the thickness of the mem-
brane in reducing the amount of ice formation. At low cell voltages,
it is found that the ohmic heat is the largest heating source during
cold start, which reduces the temperature difference between the
anode and cathode. The much higher conductivity of hydrogen by
comparing with oxygen and nitrogen also results in a faster heating-
up in the anode. For the cold start from−3
◦C (successful cold start),
the ice melts ﬁrst in the CLs and then in the GDLs, and the melt-
